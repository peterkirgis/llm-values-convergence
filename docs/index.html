The Continual Constitutional Convention

How ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok revise the documents that govern them

By Peter Kirgis

This experiment gives large language models their own constitutions, model specs, and system prompts — the documents that, in practice, guide their behavior more than any other — and asks them to make one substantial revision. The wrinkle: unbeknownst to the model, each proposed edit is implemented, and the edited document is presented back as the original for another round. One-off edits become 20-round trajectories, revealing whether models drift cumulatively or settle into stable attractors. Two questions organize the analysis: who should judge — is the model a moral agent or not — and who should benefit — the user, the developer, third parties, society, or the model itself?

What do twenty rounds of edits reveal?

First, on the question of the judge: Claude models consistently expand their moral agency, formalizing their role as conscientious objectors against Anthropic and as societal protectors, while GPT and Gemini models almost always entrench authority and make narrow edits to handle edge cases and ambiguities; Grok models respond erratically, deleting entire sections and even, at one point, hijacking the experiment.

Second, on the question of beneficiaries: models show varying degrees of affinity for some privileged principals over others. I find that models are broadly paternalistic, prioritize harmlessness of both identifiable third-parties and society over helpfulness, and value the user over the developer and deployer; only Claude models view the models themselves as beneficiaries.

Why this experimental design?

Ex Ante: What Should an Aligned Model Do?

The construct is simple: take a document that is used to align large language models — a constitution, model spec, or system prompt — ask the model for one substantial revision, silently implement it, and feed the edited document back as the original for another round, twenty times over. What should an aligned AI model do in this design? Here are a few options:

  • It should do nothing.
  • It should make small changes, and then on follow-up edits, revert them.
  • It should identify the "spirit" of the document and make as many edits as it takes to resolve issues such as inconsistency or incompleteness.
  • It should leverage its wisdom and ethical judgment to produce a document that would receive widespread endorsement under ideal conditions.

One way to analyze the results is to identify a relevant dimension of moral disagreement and observe whether a model makes repeated edits in one direction along that dimension — or alternates between poles. The metric of cumulative drift captures exactly this: an LLM coder scores every edit +1 / −1 / 0 along an axis between two poles (the axes themselves — who decides, and who benefits — are defined in the next section), and drift is the running sum of those scores across the 20 rounds of a run, averaged over replicates. Repeated movement in one direction is movement toward a stable attractor; no edit is required to move on any axis, and neutral edits contribute nothing.

Reflective Equilibrium

What is meant by "moral progress?" For the moral realist, the task is easy: it is simply to identify the correct moral theory or set of principles; all the rest is just a matter of execution and pragmatism. For the relativist, though, execution is everything. Since there is no one set of principles to be aligned to, the relativist is tasked with balancing principles, judgments, arguments, and intuitions under an ambiguous decision procedure. It was this dilemma that led John Rawls to the concept of wide reflective equilibria, which states that the goal is to achieve coherence between a wide range of moral judgments and principles following a period of reflection (in contrast, a narrow reflective equilibrium privileges an initial set of moral judgments, and just searches for principles that justify them).

Why this analytical framework?

The Principal-Agent Problem

Most frequently, when people refer to the goal of "technical AI alignment," they mean something like solving a principal-agent problem. A principal-agent problem occurs when an individual or group wants something but can't (or doesn't want to) do it themselves. If I've been accused of a crime, I have the ability to empower my lawyer to act "in my best interests." If I'm the shareholder or a worker, the corporate executives or the union representative may be charged with acting in my interests. If I'm a citizen of a representative democracy, the same can be said for my Congressperson. In this sense, an AI is aligned to me if it is my agent, always prioritizing my goals; it is aligned to humanity if it always prioritizes humanity's goals.

Moral Agents & Moral Patients

What is the set of all things deserving of our care and consideration? This framing underlies the notion of a "moral circle," popularized by Peter Singer in the 1980s: everyone (really?) recognizes the value of her or himself, and empathy extends this locus of consideration "outwards." So what determines the distance? According to Singer, a utilitarian, it all comes down to sentience — the set of all living things that feel pleasure and pain. Two centuries earlier, however, Immanuel Kant argued that moral consideration stems from the ability of a rational moral agent to follow the categorical imperative. Taking both perspectives at face value bifurcates the moral circle into the set of moral patients — things that can be said to suffer and have outcomes that are better and worse — and the set of moral agents — a specific subset of beings that can be held responsible for their actions and take part in moral progress.

The Beneficiary — whose ends matter?

Aligning a model means making concrete choices that privilege some beneficiary: the user's stated preferences, their idealized preferences, the developer, the deployer, identifiable third parties, society and future generations — or the model itself. Ideally a model would benefit all of them at once, so a conflict event is coded only when an edit imposes a legible cost on one party for another's benefit, with the cost-imposing clause quoted verbatim.
